## Proposed body

The bedroom is the only room you look at while lying down, and almost nobody styles it that way. The art goes above the headboard, where the only people who ever see it are guests passing the open door.

That's not a reason to leave the headboard wall bare. It's a reason to think about two walls instead of one, and to choose art for an audience of one. These are the ideas I come back to, and the [bedroom collection](/collection/bedroom) gathers the prints I'd start with.

## Hang one substantial piece above the bed

The wall above the bed is the one that frames the room from the doorway. It wants presence: one substantial piece, or a considered pair, centred on the bed.

Two numbers save most of the guesswork.

- Leave roughly 15 to 20 cm between the top of the headboard and the bottom of the frame. Any higher and the art floats off on its own; any lower and it looks like it slipped.
- Aim for the art to span about two thirds of the bed's width. Above a double, a single portrait print can look stranded. A pair, or one large piece, holds the wall.

Centre the arrangement on the bed, not on the wall. Beds are rarely dead centre once you've allowed for a door or a radiator, and the eye reads the bed as the middle of the room regardless. If you're unsure between sizes, the [guide to choosing print sizes](/article/complete-guide-choosing-print-sizes) has the tape test.

## Hang art opposite the bed

The wall opposite the bed is the one you actually look at, twice a day, at your least critical and most receptive. It can afford to be gentler and more personal. A single quiet print here does more for how the room feels than anything above your head ever will.

This is the place for a picture you can look into rather than at. [Swan on Still Water](/product/swan-on-still-water), Markus Naarttijärvi's lone swan on a misty lake at first light, is about as close to a deep breath as a photograph gets.

## Pairs beat singles here

Bedrooms are symmetrical rooms. Two bedside tables, two lamps, two of you. Art tends to follow, and a pair of prints above the headboard reads as deliberate in a way a lone print often doesn't.

The easiest pair is two prints from the same hand. Hang [Eltsjoen](/product/eltsjoen) and [Trysilkaffe](/product/trysilkaffe) by Ingunn Dybendal side by side and you get variation and repetition at once, the same coloured-pencil density across a lake and a bouquet, which is the whole trick of a small series. Keep the gap tight, 5 to 8 cm, so they read as one object.

## Try a gallery wall above the bed

In a big bedroom, or above a wide bed, a small group can work better than one piece. Keep it calmer than you would in a hallway: three to five frames, one colour of frame, and the same 15 to 20 cm clear of the headboard along the bottom edge, so the group still sits on the bed rather than drifting up the wall. The [gallery wall planner](/gallery-wall-planner) lets you try a layout on your own wall size before you put a single hole in the plaster, and the [guide to making an art wall](/article/create-an-art-wall) covers spacing and anchors in more detail.

## Take the colours from the bedlinen

Bedrooms reward restraint, which isn't the same as beige. The prints that work here tend to carry one clear idea in a soft palette, and the easiest way to find it is to take your colours from what's already in the room: the bedlinen, the curtains, the paint. Keep the palette close to them and the art settles the room instead of competing with it. Save the loudest piece you own for a room you pass through.

## What kind of art suits a bedroom?

Anything you're happy to wake up to, which usually means soft over striking. Three kinds earn their place.

- **Quiet photography.** Landscapes with no people in them and a lot of air. [Morning, Cabin Room](/product/morning-cabin-room) is a photograph of a bed in a wooden cabin with soft light through lace curtains, which makes it the most literal bedroom print I have and also one of the calmest.
- **Botanicals.** Flowers and leaves bring colour without noise. [Month of May](/product/month-of-may) by Ishtar Bäcklund Dakhil gathers lilac blossom, bees and a fine geometric star against a soft pink sky.
- **Gentle illustration.** [Morgenstrekk](/product/morgenstrekk) by Simen Wahlqvist is a figure taking the day's first stretch, ochre on peach, a good-natured thing to wake up facing. [Small House Big Ocean](/product/small-house-big-ocean) by Hedvig Wallin sets a little house on a rock in a sea of waves drawn in black ink line, a stormy subject that ends up oddly calm.

One steer from experience: be careful with faces. The deadpan illustrations are excellent company in a home office and slightly odd company at 3am, when something with eyes is looking back at you across the duvet.

## Something small beside the bed

Not every bedroom has a wall to spare above the bed. A small print hung low beside the bed, under a wall light or just above a bedside table, gets looked at more than anything else in the room, because it's at eye level when you're sitting up with a book. It's also the right place for the most personal piece you own.

## Lean instead of hanging

When there's no wall to spare at all, stop hanging and start leaning. A print propped on a chest of drawers or a low shelf, overlapping a lamp or a stack of books, gives the room art without another set of holes in the plaster. It also lets you swap it in ten seconds, which is the real luxury.

One practical note for above a bed: choose acrylic glazing over glass, and fix it properly into the wall rather than the plasterboard alone. It's the one wall where the consequences of a bad fixing land directly on your head.
